\section{Precision}
\label{sec:precision}

\begin{question}
  \textbf{What do FP8 and MXFP8 buy, and do they train the same?} Low precision doubles the matmul rate but not the network: does the gain measured on one node hold at scale? Does the loss follow BF16, within its noise?
\end{question}

\paragraph{Speed (C-speed), answer.} Little, so far: MXFP8 speeds up Qwen by $\times$\TriQwenFpOne{} on 1 node and $\times$\TriQwenFpTop{} on \TriQwenFpTopNodes{} nodes, and GLM-5 by $\times$\TriGlmFpMx{} at 13 nodes, against nearly $\times$2 on a matrix multiply alone. FP8 is really in use. Matrix multiplies are just too small a share of a step. Qwen's step times here are throttled by the CPU clock drop (\cref{sec:machine}); GLM-5's are not.

\paragraph{What was measured.} BF16 and MXFP8 for Qwen3.8-27B at \texttt{hsdp8} and 24k tokens per GPU (the pinned TorchTitan has no other FP8 recipe); BF16, FP8 tensorwise, FP8 blockwise and MXFP8 for GLM-5 at \texttt{pp13-ep8} and batch 256 (\cref{fig:precision_speed}). Qwen ran three times at 1 node, with BF16 runs \TriQwenFpOneSpread\,\% apart, as large as the gain itself. GLM-5 has one run per recipe, over 19 steps.

\textbf{FP8 is real.} A standalone $8192^2$ matmul on B200 reaches 2,991--3,053\,TFLOP/s in FP8 and 1,580--1,637 in BF16, with the math library's log naming the 8-bit type. In GLM-5, the same logs confirm FP8 for tensorwise and blockwise: 97\,\% of matrix-multiply FLOPs on two of the three ranks logged, 76\,\% on the third, whose output layer stays in BF16. On B200, blockwise runs as MXFP8 matrix multiplies. MXFP8 itself is identified only from kernel names, because its library logs were lost.

\textbf{Where the gain goes.} One profiled GLM-5 step, on one of the 3 ranks logged: matrix multiplies take 6.25\,s of about 48\,s in BF16, and 4.28\,s with quantization in MXFP8, a saving of 2.0\,s, which is most of the 2.64\,s between the two recipes' mean steps. The rest does not change: 12--20\,s in NCCL and 22--30\,s in other kernels, across those ranks. That is one step on three ranks, so it explains the size of the gain but is not a measurement of it.

\textbf{Qwen depends on recompute.} Under full recompute, MXFP8 gives $\times$\TriQwenFpFull{} on 1 node, against $\times$\TriQwenFpOne{} under selective. A likely reason (inferred): the selective recompute list saves no \texttt{\_scaled\_mm} output, so MXFP8 recomputes its matrix multiplies while BF16 keeps them. Adding them to the list ran out of memory.

\textbf{GLM-5 over a longer run.} Over 500 steps, BF16 steps average \TriGlmLongStepBf\,s and MXFP8 \TriGlmLongStepMx\,s (medians, $\times$\TriGlmLongFp), against \TriGlmStepBf\,s for BF16 in the 19-step run. BF16 slows down once the learning rate starts to move the weights and its memory rises to \TriGlmLongMemBf\,GiB, and the cause is not measured. These two runs are on different nodes, so this gain is less clean than the 19-step one.

\textbf{Memory.} Qwen's peak falls from \TriQwenMemBf{} to \TriQwenMemMx\,GiB with MXFP8 under selective recompute, but that comes from selective recompute, not from FP8: with full recompute both peak at \TriQwenFullMem\,GiB. GLM-5's peak rises with FP8, from \TriGlmMemBf{} to \TriGlmMemTensor--\TriGlmMemMx\,GiB (+\TriGlmMemPctTensor\ to +\TriGlmMemPctMx\,\%), and we do not know why.

\textbf{Still to come:} Qwen at 39 nodes; GLM-5 at 39 nodes.

\paragraph{Loss (C-loss), answer.} \textbf{Not settled.} Whether MXFP8 trains the same as BF16 depends so far on the seed, and 500 steps are too few to say which seed is typical. On Qwen, MXFP8 ends \TriLossSeedOneLo{} to \TriLossSeedOneHi{} above BF16 at seed 1234, and \TriLossSeedTwoLo{} and \TriLossSeedThreeLo{} at seeds 4321 and 5678 (mean loss over steps 401--500). On GLM-5, with one run of each and no noise runs yet, it ends \TriLossGlmLast{} above.

\paragraph{What was measured.} The recipes train for 500 steps at 13 nodes on the same FineWeb data with a realistic learning rate, at \texttt{hsdp8} and 24k tokens per GPU for Qwen, \texttt{pp13-ep8} for GLM-5. The loss is logged at each step and runs are compared on that index (\cref{fig:precision_loss,tab:loss_gaps}). On Qwen, a seed sets the data order as well as the initialization, so the seeds differ in what the model reads. On GLM-5 the second seed does not reshuffle the data.

\textbf{Against the noise, on Qwen.} Two runs at the same seed differ by at most \TriLossSameMax, so the seed 1234 gap is well above that noise. It is not above the difference between seeds: BF16 at seed 1234 ends \TriLossOtherMax{} below BF16 at the other two seeds, while MXFP8 at all three seeds ends within \TriLossOtherMxMax{} of each other. Most of the gap at seed 1234 therefore comes from that one BF16 trajectory; the nodes do not explain it, since the crossed runs give the same gap on both sets.

\textbf{What the curves share.} In all three seeds MXFP8 is ahead early, then the gap rises at a similar rate until the end, passing zero at about step 250--300 at seed 1234 and only at about step 475--500 at the other two seeds (\cref{fig:precision_loss}). At step 500 the gaps are \TriLossSeedOneEnd, \TriLossSeedTwoEnd{} and \TriLossSeedThreeEnd. If that rise continued, MXFP8 would end behind at every seed; that is an extrapolation, and 500 steps cannot tell. A longer run would.

\textbf{On GLM-5}, the gap also grows, to \TriLossGlmEnd{} at step 500, in the same direction as Qwen's. The two runs are on different nodes and there are no repeats, so no noise band exists; we can't say whether this is more than noise. GLM-5's learning rate starts at 0 and warms up over 50 steps, so its first steps say little about a recipe.

\textbf{Still to come:} GLM-5 BF16 at the same seed and at another seed (the noise band), then perhaps FP8 tensorwise and blockwise.
